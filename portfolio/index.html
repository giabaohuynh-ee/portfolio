About

The question I keep coming back to: how cheaply can you make a surface conductive without giving up the properties that made it useful in the first place?

A copper trace on a rigid board is a solved problem. A conductive trace on fabric that survives bending, washing, and a human body is not. In Dr. Takshi's Bio-Organic Electronics Lab I work on one narrow piece of that: converting lignin into laser-induced graphene, then electroplating copper on top, and finding the laser setting where the result is conductive enough to compete with a normal PCB trace.

The rest of my work sits on the other side of the same problem. If you are going to put electronics on a person, something has to read the sensors, decide what matters, and respond fast enough to be useful. That is where the embedded projects come from.

I am a second-year student at USF, and I also lead the hardware team for ACM's project hub and run electronics workshops for IEEE RAS.

Laser-induced graphene on fabric

Research Volunteer, Bio-Organic Electronics Lab, USF

  • Ran more than 50 trial samples on an integrated laser-etching and copper-electroplating system to find the laser power that converts lignin to conductive graphene most reliably.
